\begin{abstract}
% TODO: finalize once the human-vs-AI-panel comparison experiment (Section
% "Evaluation") has been run. Draft abstract below states the system and its
% verified properties honestly; it does not yet claim an empirical result.
Expert-elicitation instruments, the Best-Worst Method (BWM), the Analytic
Hierarchy Process (AHP), and hierarchical variants of BWM, are the standard
tool for weighting decision criteria in specialized technical domains such
as construction, wind-energy asset management, and blockchain-trust design.
Assembling a sufficiently large and diverse \emph{human} expert panel is
often the actual bottleneck, not the method itself. We present Symphysis,
an open-source engine that runs these instruments against a panel of
individually configured AI agents, alongside or in place of a human panel.
Every agent is fully specified by one portable, \texttt{did:key}-identified
Agent Card, so a panel is reproducible from a single JSON file per agent
rather than a set of ad hoc prompts nobody can rerun. Symphysis enforces,
rather than merely documents, each agent's declared permissions and
guardrails: a schema-validation and denylist-scan pass runs on every
response, every agent is sampled multiple times before a response counts
toward a result, and a QA-precheck flags fabricated source claims before
they enter the solved posterior. Its Bayesian hierarchical BWM solver is
verified against Mohammadi and Rezaei's published reference implementation,
with one real discrepancy found and corrected in the process. A separate
pooling method, HAWC-BWM, combines a human-panel posterior and an
AI-panel posterior via draw-wise linear pooling across an alpha
sensitivity sweep, letting the two panels' agreement and disagreement be
inspected directly rather than collapsed into a single blended number.
This paper describes Symphysis's architecture and trust design, and
reports an empirical comparison of a human panel and a Symphysis AI panel
run on the same BWM survey already used to calibrate the TrustRouter
trust-scoring framework's dimension weights.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} expert elicitation \quad multi-agent systems \quad Best-Worst Method \quad Analytic Hierarchy Process \quad Bayesian hierarchical modeling \quad agent identity \quad AI trust and safety

\section{Introduction}

Weighting decision criteria via structured expert elicitation is standard
practice in multi-criteria decision analysis, but the practical constraint
is rarely the method: it is finding enough qualified human experts,
willing and able to complete a demanding elicitation instrument, in a
sufficiently narrow technical specialty. This constraint is acute in
domains this project family already works in: construction trust-routing
decisions, wind-farm asset-management priorities, and blockchain-suitability
scoring, all of which need domain-expert judgment that a generic panel
cannot supply.

A growing body of work explores having large language models stand in for,
or alongside, human survey respondents. Most of that work does not address
two questions a reviewer of a real elicitation study will ask: can the
panel be reproduced exactly, and can the agents be trusted to have
answered honestly rather than guessed or fabricated a justification. We
built Symphysis to answer both questions by construction rather than by
convention: every agent's identity, permissions, and behavioral guardrails
are declared in one portable file and enforced in code, not left as an
informal prompting convention.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An open-source, reproducible AI-panel expert-elicitation engine
    in which agent permissions and guardrails are enforced at the
    architecture level, not merely documented.
    \item A portable, \texttt{did:key}-identified Agent Card format that
    lets a panel be exactly respawned from a single JSON file per agent.
    \item A Bayesian hierarchical BWM solver verified against a published
    reference implementation, with one real discrepancy found and
    corrected during that verification.
    \item HAWC-BWM: a draw-wise linear-pooling method for combining a
    human-panel posterior and an AI-panel posterior across an alpha
    sensitivity sweep.
    \item An empirical comparison of a human panel and a Symphysis AI
    panel on a real elicitation instrument already used in this project
    family. % TODO: confirm once Section "Evaluation" is run.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Expert elicitation and multi-criteria decision analysis}
The Best-Worst Method (Rezaei, 2015) and its Bayesian hierarchical
extension (Mohammadi and Rezaei, 2020) provide the elicitation and
solving methodology this system implements; the Analytic Hierarchy
Process (Saaty, 1980) is the second instrument family supported.

\subsection{Agent identity, permission enforcement, and prompt-injection risk}
He et al. (2026, arXiv:2608.27299) show that both model-side
instruction-hierarchy defenses and a common harness-side automatic
permission reviewer can be defeated by untrusted tool-level content
engineered to be read as a legitimate instruction rather than data. This
motivates Symphysis's deterministic, non-LLM-mediated permission check
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) rather than an LLM-based intent reviewer.

\subsection{Multi-agent shared-substrate risk}
Papadopoulos et al. (2026, arXiv:2608.10218) show that a goal or idea can
propagate agent-to-agent through ordinary communication in a shared
multi-agent substrate, and that idle or under-specified agents are more
susceptible while an explicit warning in an agent's system prompt confers
near-total immunity, directly relevant to any Symphysis deployment where
multiple agents share one survey's knowledge repository.

\subsection{Adjacent AI-for-research-process systems}
Schmidgall et al. (2026, arXiv:2608.26701) and Li et al. (2025,
arXiv:2510.15624) address the related but distinct problem of AI agents
accelerating or standing in for parts of the scientific research process
more broadly (hypothesis generation through manuscript writing), rather
than expert-elicitation surveys specifically; Symphysis is narrower in
scope and, by design, sidesteps the context-window-overload and
rigid-pipeline failure modes those systems' own authors identify, since
each Symphysis agent is stateless per survey round and driven by a fixed
instrument rather than an adaptive long-horizon plan.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{The Agent Card}
Every agent is fully defined by one portable JSON file: a real
\texttt{did:key} identity, role, model and hyperparameters, an optional
RAG corpus, sampling policy, tool access, permissions (which data paths
and providers it may use), and guardrails (denylist patterns). Given this
file, plus the shared DID seed, any party can respawn an identical agent
without reading Symphysis's own source code.

\subsection{Instruments}
The instrument owns prompt construction and response schema; agents do
not know or care which instrument they are completing. \texttt{bwm}
(Best-Worst Method), \texttt{ahp} (Analytic Hierarchy Process), and
\texttt{hierarchical\_bwm} (several BWM comparisons in one response, for
a criteria tree rather than a flat list) are implemented behind a common
interface designed for further methods to be added the same way.

\subsection{Enforced permissions and guardrails}
A RAG-enabled agent's declared corpus path must match one of its card's
\texttt{permissions.data\_scopes} globs or the agent refuses to start; its
model provider must be in \texttt{permissions.allowed\_providers}. Every
response is schema-validated and rejected or re-sampled on malformed
output; a denylist regex scan catches prompt-injection markers or
secret-shaped strings even in otherwise well-formed output; every agent
is sampled multiple times, never treating a single completion as ground
truth; every raw model completion, not just the parsed answer, is logged
for audit.

\subsection{Deterministic genuineness checks}
A QA-precheck restatement is checked against ground truth, and a
\texttt{sources\_used} claim is checked against what was actually
available in the prompt, flagging a fabricated citation before it can
enter a solved result.

\subsection{Solving and pooling}
The classical BWM linear program (Rezaei, 2015) and the Bayesian
hierarchical BWM (Mohammadi and Rezaei, 2020), verified against the
original authors' reference JAGS implementation, with one real bug found
and fixed in the process, solve each panel's responses into a posterior.
HAWC-BWM combines a human-panel posterior and an AI-panel posterior via
draw-wise linear pooling across a full alpha sensitivity sweep, so
agreement and disagreement between the two panels can be inspected
directly rather than collapsed into one blended estimate.

\subsection{Design}
We will run the same BWM instrument, TrustRouter's own trust-dimension
weighting survey, through both the original human expert panel and a
Symphysis AI panel, solve both posteriors with the verified Bayesian
hierarchical BWM solver, and pool them via HAWC-BWM's draw-wise linear
pooling across an alpha sensitivity sweep.

\subsection{Expected outcome}
We expect broad directional agreement between the two panels' solved
weights, with disagreement concentrated in criteria where domain-specific
tacit knowledge (not captured in any agent's RAG corpus) matters most,
and we expect the enforced-permission and guardrail pipeline to produce
an auditable, fully reproducible trail for every AI-panel response.
